\begin{abstract}
This document describes the methodology behind our exploratory network
analysis of social media addiction (SMA) and emotional well-being among
university-aged students. Following the partial-correlation network
approach of a reference paper by \textcite{wang2025dark}, we combine the Bergen Social Media
Addiction Scale (BSMAS) with a custom five-item Emotional Well-Being scale
and estimate a single partial-correlation network on a sample of
$N = 113$ participants. We assess Cronbach's $\alpha$ for both scales,
estimate edge weights via the graphical lasso, and compute Bridge Expected
Influence (BEI) for every node. Each of these quantities is accompanied
by a bootstrapped $95\%$ confidence interval (CI), and the overall
stability of the BEI vector is further quantified by a case-dropping
Correlation Stability (CS) coefficient.
\end{abstract}

\section{Data Collection}

\subsection{Questionnaire}
Our data was collected via a questionnaire, implemented as a Google Forms
survey and distributed online. Recruitment relied on convenience sampling:
the link was shared by teaching assistants, student associations, and
student discussion groups at ETH Zurich and the University of Zurich, and
forwarded by personal contacts to a small number of partner universities
in the Netherlands, France, Germany, and the United Kingdom. Form
responses were exported as a single CSV file, which serves as the input
to the preprocessing pipeline.

\subsection{Measures}
The questionnaire collected the following items:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Demographics}: age (numeric), gender (Male / Female /
        Non Binary), education level (Bachelor / Master / PhD / Post Doc),
        and field of study (text).

    \item \textbf{Social Media Addiction (SMA)}: the six items of the
        Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale
        (BSMAS)~\parencite{andreassen2016relationship,leung2020measurement}
        (Salience, Tolerance, Mood Modification, Relapse, Withdrawal, and
        Conflict), rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = ``very rarely'',
        5 = ``very often'').

    \item \textbf{Emotional Well-Being (FEELINGS)}: a custom five-item
        scale capturing negative affect (Tired, Stressed, Sad, Anxious,
        Frustrated), rated on the same 5-point Likert scale.

    \item \textbf{Attention check (AT)}: a single forced-response item
        (``Please select option 4 out of the 5 available choices'') with
        the expected answer ``Option 4''.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Participants}
The form received $118$ raw responses. After the exclusions described in
Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}, the analytical sample contains $113$
respondents.

\section{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

The raw responses are cleaned once and saved as a separate dataset, which
is then used as the sole input to the analysis. Preprocessing consists of
two filtering steps and a categorical recoding step.

\subsection{Exclusion Criteria}
Two filters are applied to the raw responses, in this order:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Attention check}: respondents whose answer to the
        attention-check item does not match the expected response are
        excluded. This filters out inattentive or automated responses.
    \item \textbf{Age range}: respondents younger than $18$ or older than
        $29$ (inclusive bounds) are excluded so that the sample remains
        within the university-aged range targeted by the study.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Categorical Encoding}
The two non-numeric demographic items are mapped to integer codes so that
they can be processed numerically:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gender}: Male $\to 1$, Female $\to 2$, Non Binary $\to 3$.
    \item \textbf{Education}: Bachelor $\to 1$, Master $\to 2$,
        PhD $\to 3$, Post Doc $\to 4$.
\end{itemize}

The Likert items (SMA and FEELINGS) are already numeric. Two summed
scores are appended to the cleaned dataset:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BSMAS score}: row-wise sum of the six SMA items
        (theoretical range $6$--$30$).
    \item \textbf{Emotion score}: row-wise sum of the five FEELINGS items
        (theoretical range $5$--$25$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Tools}

\subsection{Python Environment}
The preprocessing and analysis pipelines are implemented in Python. The
statistical work relies on standard scientific libraries: NumPy and pandas
for data handling, SciPy for distributional quantiles and Pearson
correlation, scikit-learn~\parencite{pedregosa2011scikit} for the
covariance estimator and the graphical lasso, and Matplotlib for figures.
Network construction and the GraphML export are handled by
NetworkX~\parencite{hagberg2008networkx}.

\subsection{Visone}
Final network layouts and visual styling are produced in
Visone~\parencite{brandes2004visone}. The Python pipeline only exports a
graph file with all relevant attributes; the actual layout, colour
mapping, and edge-thickness mapping are applied in Visone.

\section{Data Analysis}

The analysis pipeline follows the partial-correlation network approach
of the reference paper~\parencite{wang2025dark}, applied to the combined
FEELINGS and BSMAS item set ($k = 11$ items in total). Together with the
$N = 113$ participants this yields a data matrix
$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{113 \times 11}$, not counting the demographic
data. Listwise deletion is applied across these items so that the same
sample is used for every step of the pipeline.

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}

\subsubsection{Samples}
For age we report the mean, median, and empirical standard deviation,
the observed minimum and maximum, and we visualise the distribution with
a histogram and a horizontal box plot, with mean, median, and
one-standard-deviation markers overlaid. For the two categorical
demographics (gender and education), category counts are reported.

\subsubsection{Scales}
For each thematic cluster (SMA and FEELINGS) the mean and empirical
standard deviation are reported for every individual item, as well as
for the corresponding summed score together with its observed range. The
number of participants meeting the established clinical BSMAS cut-off of
$24$~\parencite{luo2021bsmas} is reported as well. Histograms of every
item and of every summed score are produced for visual inspection.

\subsection{Internal Scale Consistency}

\subsubsection{Cronbach's $\alpha$}
Cronbach's $\alpha$~\parencite{cronbach1951coefficient} is computed for
each of the two psychometric scales (SMA and FEELINGS) to assess whether
their items reliably measure the same underlying construct.

\subsubsection{Bootstrapped 95\% Confidence Interval}
\label{sec:cronbach-ci}
A non-parametric bootstrap with replacement~\parencite{efron1979bootstrap}
is used to obtain a $95\%$ CI for each $\alpha$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Draw $2000$ resamples with repetition of size $N$ from the data
        matrix $\mathbf{X}$.
    \item Recompute Cronbach's $\alpha$ on each resample.
    \item Take the $2.5$\textsuperscript{th} and
        $97.5$\textsuperscript{th} percentiles of the resulting empirical
        distribution as the lower and upper CI bounds.
\end{enumerate}
The full bootstrap distribution is also retained and plotted as a histogram, together with the observed $\alpha$ and the CI bounds (Figure~\ref{fig:cronbach-ci}). Following~\textcite{george2003spss}, a Cronbach's $\alpha \geq 0.7$ is
considered acceptable, $\geq 0.8$ good, and $\geq 0.9$ excellent.

\subsection{Network Modeling}

\subsubsection{Standardisation}
Each item is standardised to zero mean and unit variance (column-wise
$z$-score), yielding a standardised data matrix
$\mathbf{X}_{\text{std}}$, before being passed to the covariance
estimator and the graphical lasso. The same standardisation is
recomputed inside every bootstrap iteration from the resampled data
matrix $\mathbf{X}$ in Sections~\ref{sec:edges-ci}
and~\ref{sec:bei-ci}, so that resampling and standardisation do not
interfere.

\subsubsection{Multivariate-Normality Diagnostic}
Our approach utilises the graphical lasso, which relies on a joint
Gaussian assumption for the item matrix. Marginal normality of each
item alone does not imply joint normality, so we check the assumption
directly through the squared Mahalanobis distance $D_M^2(\mathbf{x})$
of each participant. Under joint normality, $D_M^2$ follows a $\chi^2$
distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of items
($k = 11$ in our case)~\parencite{johnson2007applied}. We therefore:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Estimate the covariance matrix $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}$
        by fitting the sample (maximum-likelihood) covariance estimator
        to the standardised data matrix $\mathbf{X}_{\text{std}}$.

    \item Compute $D_M^2(\mathbf{x})$ for every participant using
        $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}$ on $\mathbf{X}_{\text{std}}$.

    \item Sort the distances and plot them against the corresponding theoretical $\chi^2$ quantiles (Q-Q plot), with the $y = x$ reference line overlaid (Figure~\ref{fig:qq-plot}).
\end{enumerate}
The plot is read qualitatively as a visual sanity check; no formal
acceptance or rejection rule is applied.

\subsubsection{Partial-Correlation Estimation}
A sparse precision matrix $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}$ is estimated
with the graphical lasso~\parencite{friedman2008sparse}, whose L1
penalty parameter is selected by internal cross-validation (the value
maximising the held-out log-likelihood is retained). The
sparsity-inducing penalty forces off-diagonal entries that are not
supported by the data to exactly zero, which avoids the dense,
noise-driven networks that a direct inversion of the empirical
covariance would produce. The partial-correlation matrix
$\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}$ is then obtained from the estimated precision
matrix by the standard normalisation
\[
    \hat{\rho}_{ij} = -\,\widehat{\Omega}_{ij} \big/
        \sqrt{\widehat{\Omega}_{ii}\,\widehat{\Omega}_{jj}},
\]
with the diagonal forced to $1$. Each non-zero off-diagonal entry corresponds to an edge between two items in the network, and its magnitude gives the strength of the direct association.

\subsection{Edges}

\subsubsection{Bootstrapped 95\% Confidence Interval}
\label{sec:edges-ci}
For each unordered item pair $(i, j)$ with $\hat{\rho}_{ij} \neq 0$ in
the full-sample network, we test the null hypothesis
\[
    H_0:\; \rho_{ij} = 0 \qquad \text{against} \qquad
    H_1:\; \rho_{ij} \neq 0,
\]
i.e.\ no direct association between items $i$ and $j$ after conditioning on all other items in the network. The test is carried out at the $\alpha = 0.05$ significance level (two-sided) via a non-parametric bootstrap with replacement:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Draw $100$ resamples with repetition of size $N$ from the data
        matrix $\mathbf{X}$.

    \item For each resample, re-run the full estimation pipeline
        (standardisation, graphical lasso, partial-correlation
        conversion).

    \item Take the $2.5$\textsuperscript{th} and
        $97.5$\textsuperscript{th} percentiles of the resulting bootstrap
        distribution of each edge as the lower and upper bounds of the
        $95\%$ CI for $\rho_{ij}$.
\end{enumerate}

We reject $H_0$ for an edge whose $95\%$ CI strictly excludes zero (both bounds lie on the same side of $0$) and fail to reject it otherwise. Only the edges for which $H_0$ is rejected are considered statistically significant and retained.

\subsection{Bridge Expected Influence}
For each node $i$ in one of the two communities
($C_a \in \{$FEELINGS, SMA$\}$),
BEI~\parencite{jones2021bridge} is computed on the full-sample
partial-correlation matrix as the sum of the partial correlations from
$i$ to every node in the other community $C_b$:
\[
    \widehat{\mathrm{BEI}}_i \;=\;
        \sum_{j \in C_b} \widehat{\rho}_{ij}.
\]
The sign of BEI tells us whether the node connects to the other
community mostly through positive or negative edges, and its magnitude
tells us how strongly that node, in total, bridges the two clusters.

\subsubsection{Bootstrapped 95\% Confidence Interval}
\label{sec:bei-ci}
For each node $i$ we test the null hypothesis
\[
    H_0:\; \mathrm{BEI}_i = 0 \qquad \text{against} \qquad
    H_1:\; \mathrm{BEI}_i \neq 0,
\]
i.e.\ node $i$ has no net bridging contribution to the opposite
community. The test is carried out at the $\alpha = 0.05$ significance
level (two-sided) using the same bootstrap scheme as for the edges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Draw $100$ resamples with repetition of size $N$ from the data
        matrix $\mathbf{X}$.

    \item For each resample, re-run the full estimation pipeline
        (standardisation, graphical lasso, partial-correlation
        conversion) and recompute BEI for every node.

    \item Take the $2.5$\textsuperscript{th} and
        $97.5$\textsuperscript{th} percentiles of the bootstrap
        distribution as the lower and upper bounds of the $95\%$ CI for
        $\mathrm{BEI}_i$.
\end{enumerate}
For each node, $\widehat{\mathrm{BEI}}_i$ is plotted with its $95\%$ CI as an error bar, together with the zero reference line (Figure~\ref{fig:bei}). \\

We reject $H_0$ for a node whose $95\%$ CI strictly excludes zero (both bounds lie on the same side of $0$) and fail to reject it otherwise. Only the nodes for which $H_0$ is rejected are considered statistically significant with respect to the BEI.

\subsubsection{Bootstrapped CS-Coefficient}
The stability of the BEI vector is quantified by the Correlation Stability (CS) coefficient, following~\textcite{epskamp2018tutorial}. In
contrast to the with-replacement bootstrap used above, the
CS-coefficient is based on a case-dropping subsample bootstrap that draws subsets without replacement:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item For each retention fraction
        $f \in \{0.95, 0.90, \dots, 0.05\}$, draw $100$ subsamples of
        size $\lfloor N\,f\rfloor$ without replacement from the data
        matrix $\mathbf{X}$.

    \item On every subsample, re-run the full estimation pipeline
        (standardisation, graphical lasso, partial-correlation
        conversion) and recompute the BEI vector.

    \item Compute Pearson's $r$ between the full-sample BEI vector and
        each subsample BEI vector.

    \item Record $\widehat{P}_f = P(r \geq 0.7)$, the proportion of
        subsamples whose BEI vector shows a Pearson correlation
        $\geq 0.7$ with the full-sample BEI vector. The loop is stopped
        early as soon as $\widehat{P}_f < 0.95$, since larger drops
        cannot satisfy the threshold.
\end{enumerate}

The CS-coefficient is then defined as the largest fraction of cases
that can be \emph{dropped} while still maintaining
$P(r \geq 0.7) \geq 0.95$. Because the grid is discrete, the
threshold-crossing point is obtained by linear interpolation: let
$f_{\text{pass}}$ be the smallest retention fraction whose
$\widehat{P}_f$ exceeds $0.95$ and $f_{\text{fail}}$ the largest one
that fails the threshold; we set
\[
    f_{\text{cross}} \;=\; f_{\text{fail}} \;+\;
    (f_{\text{pass}} - f_{\text{fail}}) \cdot
    \frac{0.95 - \widehat{P}_{f_{\text{fail}}}}
         {\widehat{P}_{f_{\text{pass}}} - \widehat{P}_{f_{\text{fail}}}},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{CS} \;=\; 1 - f_{\text{cross}}.
\]
$\mathrm{CS} \geq 0.5$ is considered good, $\mathrm{CS} \geq 0.25$
acceptable, and anything below is regarded as unstable. $\widehat{P}_f$ is plotted as a function of $f$, together with the $0.95$ threshold and the interpolated $1 - \mathrm{CS}$ crossing line (Figure~\ref{fig:cs-coef}).

\section{Network Visualization}

\subsection{Export Pipeline}
The estimated partial-correlation matrix is converted into an undirected
graph in two variants: one containing every non-zero edge, and one
containing only the edges whose bootstrapped $95\%$ CI excludes zero.
Each node carries a community attribute (SMA or FEELINGS). Each edge
carries three attributes derived from its partial correlation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the signed value, used to encode polarity,
    \item the absolute value, used to encode strength,
    \item a categorical sign indicator (positive / negative), used to
        drive a diverging colour scheme.
\end{itemize}
The graph is exported as a GraphML file, which is then loaded into
Visone for layout and styling.

\subsection{Visone Settings}
Both variants of the network are styled identically in Visone, using the
attributes set at export time:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Layout}: circular, with the two communities arranged
        on opposite arcs of the circle. The circular layout is chosen
        deliberately to keep the graph readable at a glance and to
        avoid the visual clustering produced by force-directed layouts.

    \item \textbf{Node colour}: bound to the community attribute, with
        one colour per cluster.

    \item \textbf{Edge colour}: bound to the sign attribute, green for
        positive partial correlations and red for negative ones.

    \item \textbf{Edge thickness}: linearly mapped from the absolute
        partial correlation, so that visually thicker edges correspond
        to stronger direct associations.

    \item \textbf{Node labels}: each node carries the item name from
        the questionnaire.
\end{itemize}
The styled networks are exported from Visone as image files for inclusion in the report and presentation (Figures~\ref{fig:network-all} and~\ref{fig:network-sig}).
